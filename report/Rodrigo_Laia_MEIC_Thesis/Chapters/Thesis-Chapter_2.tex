\section[Differences Between European Portuguese and Brazilian Portuguese]{Differences Between European Portuguese\\and Brazilian Portuguese}
\label{sec:epbp-differences}

Before introducing the modeling background, it is useful to separate the
linguistic phenomena that provide evidence for EP/BP identification from those
that require edits during rewriting. These contrasts are not limited to isolated vocabulary
substitutions. Prior literature on paraphrastic correspondences between the two varieties
shows that many differences appear as recurrent alternatives in how equivalent
meanings are expressed, spanning lexical and morphosyntactic variation \citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic}.
\citet{Cyrino_2001} also discusses contrasts involving null objects and verb phrase
ellipsis, relating them to changes in the verbal structure of BP sentences.

These observations are consistent with the quantitative analysis reported by
\citet{alves-2025-information}, which studies EP/BP variation using
information-theoretic measures. Lexical choice accounts for the largest
divergence between the two varieties, but the research also identifies systematic
grammatical preferences in subject-pronoun use, progressive constructions, and
clitic-related patterns. Analyses based on part-of-speech distributions,
dependency relations, and word-order entropy further suggest that EP and BP
share most of their syntactic backbone, while still diverging in recurrent
local preferences that are salient enough to affect automatic processing.

The following list highlights representative contrasts between the two
Portuguese varieties that are relevant to identification and rewriting:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Orthography and morphology.} Orthographic differences can provide
useful variety cues, especially in older or noisier corpora. However, many
spelling contrasts were reduced by the Portuguese Orthographic Agreement, so
forms such as \textit{projecto}, \textit{acto}, or \textit{baptismo} are less
reliable as markers in contemporary standard data \citep{costa-jussa-etal-2018-neural}.

\item \textbf{Progressive and aspectual periphrases.} EP typically uses \textit{estar a} + infinitive for the progressive (e.g.,
\textit{Ele estava a correr}), whereas BP typically uses \textit{estar} +
gerund\hfil\break (e.g., \textit{Ele estava correndo})
\citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic}.

\item \textbf{Clitic placement and word order.} EP commonly places clitics
postverbally with hyphenation in affirmative contexts (e.g., \textit{Ele
viu-me na rua}), while BP more often favors preverbal placement without
hyphenation (e.g., \textit{Ele me viu na rua})
\citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic}.

\item {\interlinepenalty=10000 \textbf{Subject pronoun realization (pronoun drop).} Both varieties allow null subjects, but BP uses overt subject pronouns more often than EP.\hfil\break For example, EP allows \textit{Fui ao supermercado ontem},
whereas BP more often uses \textit{Eu fui ao supermercado ontem}
\citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic, costa-jussa-etal-2018-neural}.\par}

\item \textbf{Pronoun preferences.} BP commonly uses \textit{você} as a
neutral or default second-person address form, whereas EP more often uses
\textit{tu}, nominal address forms, or null subjects depending on register. In
rewriting, this can create frequent pairs between EP second-person verb
morphology and BP third-person morphology with \textit{você}
\citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic, costa-jussa-etal-2018-neural}.

\item \textbf{Determiners with possessives and zero determiners.} In BP,
definite articles before possessives are often omitted (e.g., \textit{Vendi
meu carro}),\hfil\break whereas EP more typically requires them (e.g., \textit{Vendi o meu
carro}). BP also permits zero determiners more often than EP \citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic, Cyrino_2001}.

\item \textbf{Lexical and multiword preferences.} Some of the most robust
distinctions in written corpora arise from lexical choice and multiword
expressions, such as \textit{comboio}/\textit{trem},
\textit{fato}/\textit{terno}, or \textit{toda a gente}/\textit{todo o mundo}
\citep{barreiro-mota-2018-paraphrastic, costa-jussa-etal-2018-neural}.
\end{itemize}

These phenomena affect the two tasks in different ways. For variety
identification, lexical preferences and some orthographic forms often provide
strong surface evidence. For rewriting, the structural phenomena are more
important, requiring the system to decide whether to preserve the sentence, apply
a local substitution, or make a constrained syntactic change while preserving
meaning and scope.

\section{Modern Language Models}
\label{sec:modern-language-models}

This section introduces the modeling background needed for the remainder of the
document. It begins with language modeling itself
(\Cref{sec:language-models}), then describes the Transformer architecture and
the distinction between encoders and decoders (\Cref{sec:transformers}).
It then reviews the main Transformer architectures used for language tasks and
explains why encoder-decoder models are an appropriate starting point for
Portuguese variety identification and rewriting
(\Cref{sec:model-families}).

\subsection{Language Modeling}
\label{sec:language-models}

Language modeling is the task of assigning probabilities to sequences of text.
Given a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, a language model estimates
the probability of the full sequence by factorizing it into conditional
probabilities, typically of the form
$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. In practice, this means
the model learns which tokens are likely to occur in a given context, where
$x_{<t}$ denotes the tokens that precede position $t$. In autoregressive
settings, this factorization is implemented directly as next-token prediction.
Given a prefix, the model predicts the most likely continuation one token at a
time. In other self-supervised settings, the training signal is different, but
the model still uses context to reconstruct portions of text and thereby learn
regularities of the language \citep{zhao2025surveylargelanguagemodels}.

Early language models were predominantly statistical. A common example is the
$n$-gram model, which estimates the probability of a token from the previous
$n-1$ tokens by counting how often short sequences occur in a corpus. This
approach is simple and effective for capturing local regularities, but it is
limited by the fixed size of the context window. It also suffers from data
sparsity, as many possible sequences occur rarely or not at all, even in
large corpora \citep{880083,Jelinek1997StatisticalMF}.

Neural language models replaced explicit count-based statistics with learned
distributed representations \citep{10.5555/944919.944966}. Instead of treating
each context as an isolated symbolic pattern, these models learn dense token
representations and trainable functions that can generalize across related
contexts. Recurrent architectures, especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks, extended this idea to variable-length sequences and made it possible
to carry information across longer contexts, although efficient large-scale
training remained difficult \citep{Mikolov2010RecurrentNN}.

Language-modeling objectives can be applied without task-specific labels, which
supports pretraining on large unlabeled corpora before adaptation to downstream
tasks. Pretrained models therefore offer a stronger starting point than training
from scratch. The next subsection introduces the Transformer architecture, which
underlies many state-of-the-art language models and is central to this study.

\subsection{Transformers: Self-Attention, Encoders and Decoders}
\label{sec:transformers}

The central operation in the Transformer architecture is self-attention.
Self-attention allows each token in a sequence to combine information from
other positions by computing attention weights that reflect learned similarities
between token representations. Each token is first mapped to a continuous
embedding. Since self-attention is permutation-invariant, information about
token order must be added separately through positional encodings. In the
original Transformer, these are fixed sinusoidal vectors added to token
embeddings, yielding input representations
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}$, where $n$ is the sequence
length and $d_{\text{model}}$ is the hidden dimension. In practice, $n$ is
bounded by the model's context window, the maximum number of tokens it can
attend to in one forward pass \citep{NIPS2017_3f5ee243}.

Given token representations $X$, which already include positional information,
queries, keys, and values are obtained through learned linear projections,
\[
Q = XW^Q, \qquad K = XW^K, \qquad V = XW^V.
\]
The scaled dot-product attention used throughout the architecture is then given
by
\[
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) =
\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
\]
The operation replaces each token representation by a similarity-weighted
combination of the others. Multi-head attention repeats this computation with
different learned projections and concatenates the results,
allowing the model to capture several interaction patterns in parallel
\citep{NIPS2017_3f5ee243}.

Although many later architectures are based on self-attention, the original
Transformer was proposed as an encoder-decoder model for sequence-to-sequence
tasks. In the original architecture, the encoder consists of a stack of Transformer
blocks. Each block contains a
self-attention sublayer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, where
the same multilayer perceptron is applied independently to each token
representation. Residual connections add the input of each sublayer to its
output, allowing the network to refine previous representations instead of
replacing them entirely. Layer normalization supports stable training. Since
encoder self-attention is bidirectional, every position can attend to every
other position in the source sequence. The result is a sequence of contextual
representations that is useful both for text understanding and for conditioning
downstream generation \citep{NIPS2017_3f5ee243}.

The decoder uses a similar stack, with two additional mechanisms required for
generation. First, masked self-attention prevents each position from attending
to future tokens, so generation proceeds from left to right. Second,
cross-attention lets the decoder attend to encoder outputs, conditioning target
generation on the source sequence \citep{NIPS2017_3f5ee243}.

From these building blocks, modern pretrained models organize encoders and
decoders in different ways. The next subsection reviews the main families that
result from these choices and explains why the encoder-decoder architecture is
suitable for the tasks studied in this work.

\subsection{Transformer Architectures for Language Tasks}
\label{sec:model-families}

Modern pretrained language models share the Transformer building blocks
introduced above, but organize those blocks differently and pair them with
different pretraining objectives. The main families relevant to the present
work are encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder models.

Encoder-only models such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) are bidirectional encoders trained to recover
masked tokens or spans from surrounding context
\citep{devlin-etal-2019-bert}. Since each position can
attend to both left and right context, these models are especially effective at
building contextual representations for tasks such as classification, retrieval,
or span prediction. In practice, they are often adapted by attaching a
task-specific prediction head on top of the encoder. Their limitation for the
present work is that they are not generative by default, which makes them less convenient
when one wants a single model family to cover both understanding- and
generation-oriented tasks.

Decoder-only models use a single Transformer stack with causal masking and
define an autoregressive distribution over continuations
\citep{10.5555/3495724.3495883}, which makes them a natural fit for open-ended
generation. When they undergo additional adaptation through instruction tuning,
they can also become well suited to task following
\citep{chung2022scalinginstructionfinetunedlanguagemodels}. These models can be used for
classification as well, either by generating a label directly or by scoring
candidate labels according to their conditional likelihood.

Encoder-decoder models combine a bidirectional encoder with an autoregressive
decoder. The encoder constructs contextual representations of the source
sequence, and the decoder generates the target sequence while attending to that
source representation. This architecture is especially suited to conditional
generation, where the output must be produced from a given input. Machine
translation (MT) is the classical example, with the model encoding a source
sentence and decoding a target sentence. The same input-output structure can also
be used for other text tasks. In variety identification, the output is a short
label, whereas in rewriting, the output is a full sentence in the requested variety
\mbox{\citep{Raffel2019ExploringTL}}.

All three families operate over tokenized text within a finite context window
and can benefit from transfer learning through downstream fine-tuning. Modern
systems often rely on subword units, which reduce out-of-vocabulary problems
and help represent spelling or morphological variation
\mbox{\citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}}.
The relevant question is not whether these families can in principle represent
the tasks studied, but how naturally their pretraining setup aligns
with the desired use case. Encoder-only models are particularly strong for
understanding-oriented prediction and decoder-only models provide a flexible
generative interface. This work combines bidirectional rewriting and variety prediction, making
encoder-decoder models a coherent starting point for the modeling choices that
follow.

Among encoder-decoder options, T5Gemma 2 is a relevant model family for this
project. It is built by taking pretrained decoder-only Gemma checkpoints and
continuing pretraining in an encoder-decoder, text-to-text
setting with a Unifying Language Learning Paradigms (UL2)-style objective
\citep{zhang2025t5gemma2seeingreading,Tay2023}. This objective combines denoising
tasks such as span corruption with prefix-style prediction, so the model uses
bidirectional source context while retaining autoregressive generation.

For the tasks this project aims to study, the relevance of T5Gemma 2 is not
limited to its general pretrained knowledge. It is also compatible with
conditional generation and structured text targets, which are needed when a
single framework must support both sentence rewriting and short label outputs.

Two architectural choices further motivate this selection. T5Gemma 2 shares
word embeddings across the encoder and the decoder, and it replaces the usual
separation between decoder self-attention and cross-attention with a merged
attention module \citep{zhang2025t5gemma2seeingreading}. These choices are
intended to reduce duplication and improve efficiency while preserving access to
the encoded source. Recent work on adapting Gemma models from decoder-only to
encoder-decoder form further argues that this architecture offers a favorable
trade-off between richer source representations, efficient inference, and
strong downstream fine-tuning behavior \citep{Zhang2025EncoderDecoderGI}.

\section{Task Formulations for Portuguese Variety Identification and Rewriting}
\label{sec:task-formulations}



The standard formulation of variety identification is supervised
classification where, given a text segment, the system selects one label from a
small set of possible varieties, such as EP or BP
\citep{DBLP:conf/konvens/ZampieriG12, sousa2025enhancingportuguesevarietyidentification}. In a
generative model, the task can instead be represented as generation over a
restricted output space in which the decoder produces a short variety label.

Variety rewriting is naturally formulated as conditional generation. The model
receives a source sentence together with a target-variety
specification and generates an equivalent sentence in the requested variety.
The output should preserve meaning while making the changes required by
the target variety.

A shared sequence-to-sequence interface can represent both tasks within the
same model \citep{Raffel2019ExploringTL}. The two tasks rely on overlapping
variety-sensitive evidence: cues that support an EP/BP decision are often also
cues that must be preserved or modified during rewriting. Training on both
tasks can therefore allow identification data to reinforce variety-sensitive
signals while rewriting data teaches how those signals should change when a
target variety is requested, without implying that a multitask model must
outperform a dedicated single-task model on every metric
\citep{10.1023/A:1007379606734}.

In the formulations studied in this work, rewriting direction is represented through an input-variety label. One option is to
place this information in the input, so the encoder receives it together with
the source sentence. Another option is to place it in the output structure, so
the decoder is required to produce the variety label before the rewritten
sentence. These alternatives leave the underlying tasks unchanged, but change
where the control signal is introduced. The exact labels, token choices, and
dataset conversion rules are part of the experimental design and are described
in \Cref{chap:methodology}.

\section{Fine-Tuning Pretrained Language Models}
\label{sec:fine-tuning}

Pretrained models provide a strong starting point, but they do not necessarily
exhibit the behavior required by a downstream task. Even with broad linguistic
knowledge, a model may not consistently distinguish varieties, preserve meaning
during rewriting, or follow the requested target variety. Fine-tuning addresses
this gap by continuing training on data that is closer to the intended use
case.

In SFT, the model is trained on input-output examples
and its parameters are updated to increase the likelihood of the desired
targets. For a text-to-text model, both classification and
rewriting examples can be represented as sequence prediction problems
\citep{Raffel2019ExploringTL}.

SFT does not have to be carried out in a single phase. When different sources of
supervision play different roles, a model can first be adapted on broader data
and then continued on more targeted data. A staged setup is common in Neural
Machine Translation (NMT) domain adaptation, where broad parallel data is often
followed by in-domain fine-tuning
\citep{cao-etal-2021-continual, gu-etal-2022-continual}. For the present work,
the same idea motivates separating broader rewriting adaptation, more targeted
EP/BP supervision, and later reward-based optimization. The main risk is that
later phases can pull the model toward newly introduced data and weaken behavior
learned earlier \citep{gu-etal-2022-continual, gu-feng-2020-investigating}.

Another central design choice is how many parameters should be updated during
adaptation. Full fine-tuning updates the entire pretrained model. Updating all
weights gives the optimizer maximum flexibility and can be useful when the target task or domain
differs substantially from pretraining, but it is expensive in memory and
computation, and may overfit when the supervision is narrow. Parameter-Efficient
Fine-Tuning (PEFT) instead freezes most or all of the pretrained base model and
learns a much smaller set of task-specific parameters. Adapter-based methods
insert small trainable modules into the network while keeping the original
model fixed, allowing task adaptation with far fewer updated parameters than
full fine-tuning \citep{pmlr-v97-houlsby19a, pfeiffer-etal-2021-adapterfusion}.

LoRA is a widely used PEFT method. It implements
parameter efficiency by learning low-rank updates to selected weight matrices
rather than by inserting separate adapter layers. If a pretrained weight matrix
is denoted by \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\), LoRA keeps \(W\) frozen and
learns an update \(\Delta W = BA\), where \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\),
\(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), and \(r\) is much smaller than \(d\) and
\(k\). The effective adapted weight is \(W + \Delta W\), but only the
low-rank factors are trained \citep{hu2022lora}. In the common LoRA
parameterization, the new branch is scaled by \(\alpha / r\) before being added
to the frozen base projection. The rank \(r\) controls the capacity of the
learned update. Smaller ranks impose a stronger bottleneck, whereas larger
ranks allow a more expressive adaptation. The scaling factor \(\alpha\)
controls the magnitude of the LoRA contribution, and dropout can be applied to
the adapter branch as regularization.

Another LoRA design choice is which Transformer projections receive low-rank
updates. Early LoRA work often focused on attention projections for parameter
efficiency \citep{hu2022lora}, while later work showed that broader coverage
can be useful when stronger task adaptation is required. In particular,
Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA)
reports that adapting all Transformer linear layers is important for recovering
performance comparable to strong full-fine-tuning baselines
\citep{NEURIPS2023_1feb8787}. Broader coverage allows the adapters to modify
both attention interactions and feed-forward transformations while the
pretrained weights remain frozen. This distinction is relevant to the
experimental setup in Chapter 5, where LoRA is applied to both attention and
feed-forward projections.



\section{Reinforcement Learning and Reward-Based Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:rl-reward-ft}

Supervised fine-tuning optimizes a model to reproduce reference outputs token
by token. Token-level supervision is useful when a reference target is
available, but some later training objectives are easier to express as
preferences or scalar scores over complete outputs. A generated sentence may be
fluent while failing to follow an instruction, preserve meaning, respect a
target variety, or avoid an undesirable behavior. Reward-based fine-tuning
addresses this limitation by assigning a score to one or more candidate outputs
and updating the model so that higher-scoring outputs become more likely
\citep{10.5555/3600270.3602281}.

A useful abstraction for this setting is the contextual bandit view
\citep{10.5555/3312046,Lattimore_Szepesvári_2020}. Classical multi-armed
bandits model repeated decisions between actions whose reward distributions are
initially unknown. After each decision, the agent observes only the reward of
the action it selected. There is no long-term planning and no state transition
caused by the action. Contextual bandits extend this setting by conditioning
the decision on side information. At each round, a context \(X\) is sampled from
a distribution \(\mathcal{D}\), the agent chooses an action
\(a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid X)\), and observes a scalar reward \(r(X,a)\). The
objective is to learn a policy \(\pi\) that maximizes expected reward,
\[
J(\pi) =
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \mathcal{D}}
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid X)}
\left[r(X,a)\right].
\]

In reward-based fine-tuning for language models, the context \(X\) corresponds
to the conditioning information given to the model, such as an instruction and
a source sentence. The action is not a single symbolic choice, but a complete
generated output \(Y = (Y_1,\ldots,Y_{|Y|})\). A language model parameterized by
\(\theta\) defines the following autoregressive policy over complete outputs:
\[
\pi_\theta(Y \mid X) =
\prod_{t=1}^{|Y|}
\pi_\theta(Y_t \mid X, Y_{<t}).
\]
After sampling \(Y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid X)\), the output is evaluated by
a scoring function, such as a reward model, verifier, preference scorer,
automatic metric, or task-specific evaluator. The score is an outcome-level
reward \(r(X,Y)\), and the contextual-bandit objective for language-model
policy optimization becomes
\[
J(\pi_\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \mathcal{D}}
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid X)}
\left[r(X,Y)\right].
\]

The objective above can be optimized from different forms of feedback.
Pairwise-preference methods such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) use
preferred and rejected outputs for the same input and increase the relative
probability of the preferred output \citep{10.5555/3666122.3668460}. In
sampled-output optimization, the model instead generates candidate outputs,
assigns them scalar rewards, and updates the policy according to the rewards
obtained by those candidates.

Within sampled-output optimization, group-relative methods compare several
candidates generated for the same input and convert their rewards into relative
advantages \citep{Shao2024DeepSeekMathPT}. If a group contains \(C\) candidates
with rewards \(r_1,\ldots,r_C\), a standardized group-relative advantage can be
written as
\[
\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma_r + \epsilon},
\qquad
\bar{r} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{C} r_j,
\qquad
\sigma_r =
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{C}(r_j-\bar{r})^2
}.
\]
A candidate receives a positive advantage when it scores above the group
average and a negative advantage when it scores below it. The optimization
signal therefore depends on how a candidate performs relative to alternatives
for the same input rather than on the absolute scale of the reward.

Once generated candidates can be scored, the next design question is what the
reward should measure. In
language-variety rewriting, an output may need to be fluent, faithful to the
source, stylistically appropriate, and aligned with a requested constraint.
Recent work on language-model alignment studies how multiple reward
models can be transformed and combined when optimizing for several properties
at once \citep{pmlr-v235-wang24ay}. A common approach combines reward
components into a scalar objective,
\[
R(X,Y) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \lambda_m r_m(X,Y),
\]
where \(r_m\) are component rewards and \(\lambda_m\) are weights controlling
their relative importance. Reward shaping can also transform or augment these
components, for example by normalizing them, comparing them to a baseline, or
adding penalties for undesirable behaviors.
